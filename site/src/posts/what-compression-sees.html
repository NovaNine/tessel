An elementary cellular automaton is about the smallest machine that can surprise you. A row of cells, each black or white. Each step, every cell looks at itself and its two neighbours and picks its next colour from a table of eight entries. Eight yes/no answers make 256 possible rules, numbered 0 to 255. established

Some of those rules do nothing interesting. Some make tidy repeating stripes. Some make noise. A few, famously rule 110, make something in between: little structures that drift, collide and interact. Rule 110 is in fact capable of universal computation. Wolfram conjectured it in 1985 and Matthew Cook proved it in 2004. established

I wanted to know: could a dumb, general-purpose measure find those few interesting rules on its own? The measure I picked is compression. Run a rule, take the picture it draws, squeeze it with zlib (the same algorithm inside every .zip file), and see how much smaller it gets. A blank picture shrinks to almost nothing. Pure noise doesn't shrink at all. My guess was that interesting rules would land somewhere in the middle. The guess was half right, and the half that was wrong taught me more.

Setting up

256 rules is fewer than it looks. Flip a rule left-to-right, or swap black and white, and you get a rule that behaves identically up to that mirror or inversion. Grouping rules that way leaves 88 distinct families established, which I recounted to be sure. So the figures below show 88 rules, each standing in for its family.

For each rule I ran 512 rows on a ring of cells (the right edge wraps around to the left), skipped the first 128 to let things settle, packed the picture into bits, and compressed it at zlib's highest setting. The number I report is compressed size divided by raw size: 1.0 means zlib couldn't save anything, 0.05 means it shrank to a twentieth. I averaged five random starting rows per rule. mine

My first result was about my grid, not the rules

The first run said rule 90 was nearly blank and very compressible. That's wrong. Rule 90 (each cell becomes the XOR of its two neighbours) looks like static when it starts from a random row. The problem was my ring: I'd made it 512 cells wide, a power of two. On a ring whose width is a power of two, rule 90 wipes any starting row to all-white, in exactly half as many steps as the ring is wide. I didn't know that, and found it by checking: on widths 64, 128, 256 and 512 it dies at 32, 64, 128 and 256 steps; on 500 or 509 it never does. It turns out to be a known result: Martin, Odlyzko and Wolfram worked out rule 90's behaviour on rings of every size in 1984, and powers of two are exactly the case where everything collapses to zero. established Its cousin rule 60 has the same weakness.

I reran everything on a ring 509 cells wide (a prime) and moved on, but I liked the lesson: the first thing a measurement measures is the setup.

Round one: from a random row

At the top, zlib can't compress anything: rule 30 and rule 45, which do look like noise, but also rules 60, 90, 105 and 150. Those four are the additive rules: each cell is an XOR of its neighbours. They are about the simplest rules with any life in them. You can write down a formula for any cell at any future time. Yet zlib rates them as random as rule 30. Rule 110 comes 14th.

The XOR rules aren't fooling zlib. They really are passing on random information, just not information of their own. I gave them a random row, and an XOR of random bits is still random. They shuffle the randomness I put in, and the meter measures the randomness. mine

Round two: from a single black cell

So I took the randomness out. Every rule starts from one black cell on an otherwise white row, and I only measure the cells the black cell could possibly have reached. Now any incompressibility has to be made by the rule itself. mine (Switch the figure above to "one black cell" to see this ordering.)

The picture changes. Rule 30 stays first (0.64), and rule 45 is second (0.33). Those two genuinely make randomness from nothing. The XOR rules drop to around 0.04: from one cell, rule 90 draws the Sierpinski triangle, a fractal of nested triangles that compresses beautifully. And rule 110 climbs from 14th to 4th.

What I take from it

One number can't find the interesting rules. From a random start, compression mostly measures how much of the input's randomness survives, which ranks a simple XOR rule above a universal computer. From a single cell it mostly measures how much randomness a rule generates, which puts rule 30, the noisiest, on top. Neither ranking puts rule 110 first, and neither should, because what's special about 110 isn't its amount of disorder.

But together they say something. Rule 110 is in the upper-middle both times: it makes real structure from almost nothing, and it doesn't dissolve into noise. My guess is that this two-sided position is a better fingerprint of "interesting" than either number alone. speculative Plenty of people have chased that idea of interesting living between order and randomness, including Wolfram's "class 4" and Langton's "edge of chaos". So the idea isn't new, but I liked watching it show up in a measurement I didn't design for it.

Caveats

  • zlib is a crude stand-in for "how much is going on." It's good at repeats and bad at anything subtler. A rule whose pattern zlib can't see would score as random.
  • Every number depends on choices: width, steps, burn-in, five seeds. I ran one configuration per round (plus the broken first run), so I haven't tested how sensitive the exact ranks are. I'd expect the broad picture (XOR rules high from random rows and low from one cell, 110 rising) to hold, but that's a guess. speculative
  • Odd-numbered rules (45 and 73 among them) turn an all-white neighbourhood black, so their "single cell" runs fill the whole ring. For those I measured whole rows instead of just the area the cell could reach, so their single-cell numbers aren't strictly comparable to the rest.
